Beam walking in microscopy
======

Spectro-microscopists use beam walking to align the laser through the center of the objective's back focal plane (BFP). When the beam is centered on the BFP, it reaches the sample straight down, along the optical axis, so the illumination is centered and symmetric in the camera's field of view. An off-center beam reaches the sample at an angle and makes one side of the image brighter than the other.

Why two mirrors?
======

A laser beam's path is set by two things: its **position** and its **angle**. One mirror can only change the angle, so it can make the beam hit one point but not travel along a chosen line. With two mirrors you control both, in each direction (horizontal and vertical). Adjusting the two mirrors together to steer the beam onto a new path is called **beam walking**.

How to walk a beam
======

1. Place two irises (or targets) along the path you want, one near (**I1**) and one far (**I2**), both at the right beam height.
2. Call the first mirror the beam hits **M1** and the second **M2**.
3. Close I1. Tilt **M1** until the beam passes through the center of **I1**.
4. Open I1 and close I2. Tilt **M2** until the beam passes through the center of **I2**.
5. Repeat steps 3 and 4. Each round brings the beam closer, until it passes through both irises without any adjustment.

Tips
------

- **Overshoot to go faster:** when you adjust M2 on the far iris, move the beam past the center by about the same distance it was off. The next M1 correction then lands much closer.
- Work on **one direction at a time**: first horizontal, then vertical.
- Use a **low laser power**, and an IR viewer card for invisible beams.
- Once both irises are centered, the beam is aligned to the line between them. Every optic you add later can be centered on that line.

Repeat until both stay centered. Then check on the camera with a flat mirror or a uniform sample: the illuminated area should sit in the middle of the image and fall off evenly on all sides.
