The equations behind it

For a Gaussian beam with 1/e² diameter D, focused by a lens of focal length f (paraxial, aberration-free).

Spot size at the focus
2w0 = 4 λ fπ D

Shorter f or a wider input beam D gives a smaller spot. Longer wavelengths give bigger spots.

Beam radius near the focus
w(z) = w0 √1 + (z / zR)²

At z = ±zR, the beam is √2 wider than at the waist and the area doubles.

Rayleigh range
zR = π w0²λ

How far the beam travels from the waist before it grows by √2. A small waist spreads out fast.

Depth of focus · put it together
DOF = 2zR = 8 λ f²π D²

The spot grows with f, but the depth of focus grows with f².

Short focal length

Tight spot, short depth of focus

  • Steep cone, high NA
  • Small 2w₀: high intensity, fits small detectors and pinholes
  • Stays focused only briefly, so alignment is sensitive
  • More spherical aberration at steep angles
Long focal length

Bigger spot, long depth of focus

  • Gentle cone, low NA
  • Larger 2w₀: lower peak intensity
  • Stays focused over a long distance, so alignment is forgiving
  • Less aberration, a cleaner focus
w₀ ∝ f · DOF ∝ f² · halve f → spot ½, depth of focus ¼ (and vice versa)

Assumptions: ideal TEM₀₀ Gaussian beam, D measured at the 1/e² intensity points, thin lens, no aberrations (M² = 1). Real beams with M² > 1 give a waist about M² times larger. The 6″ and 10″ lenses are shown as 150 mm and 250 mm.
